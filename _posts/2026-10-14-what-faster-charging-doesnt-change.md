Last December I wrote that [the 20-minute charging window](/blog/2025/12/29/20-minute-problem.html) wasn’t going away, and that physics wouldn’t allow it. Two announcements from the past few weeks sent me back to reread that post.

## Two Announcements

A national charging network previewed equipment rated up to 750 kW. It expects that hardware to charge the fastest-charging vehicles sold in the U.S. in about ten minutes once deployment begins in 2027.

The following week, a Midwest convenience chain announced its first fast chargers, planned for six of its newest stores. All six have expanded food and beverage, seating, and Wi-Fi. Its team said they were thinking beyond the charger, about how to make a 20 to 30 minute charge convenient and enjoyable for guests.

One company is working to shorten the stop. The other is investing in making it worth staying for. I think both are right, and I think they’re describing the same stop from two ends.

## Was I Wrong About Twenty Minutes?

I think I was too confident. Faster charging is better for drivers, and we want it. Nobody on a road trip has ever wished the charge took longer.

I bought my first electric vehicle in 2014, and a charging session has become much faster since then. The network behind the 750 kW preview says the charge rates it sees have risen nearly 20% in the past two years. I see no reason for that to stop, and high-power charging is part of our own plans.

The ten-minute figure describes the best case, though. It needs a car that can accept that much power, and very few cars on sale today can take more than 350 kW. The network itself notes that real charging times depend on the vehicle, the condition of the battery, the temperature, and how full the battery is on arrival. Cars also stay in service for many years, so the mix of vehicles pulling into a charging location changes slowly.

If I wrote that post today, I’d put less weight on physics and more on the cars already on the road. For most drivers, I expect most stops to stay closer to twenty or thirty minutes for a good while yet.

## Ten Minutes Is Still a Visit

Suppose the best case becomes the normal case. Ten minutes is still longer than most fill-ups at a gas pump. It’s also about the length of the things people stop for anyway: a restroom, a coffee, a stretch.

The charge is only part of the stop, too. A driver has to pull in, find an open charger, start the session, walk to wherever they’re going, and walk back. If starting the session takes three minutes of downloading and signing in, a ten-minute charge becomes a thirteen-minute stop. That’s one reason charging at Rangeway locations is planned to be app-free.

In hotels, the shortest interactions carry the most weight. Arrival takes a few minutes and shapes how a guest feels about the entire stay. A short visit has no slack in it. If the restroom is locked, the walk to the door is long, or the line for coffee is slow, there’s no time left to recover.

A shorter stop doesn’t make the place matter less. It makes every detail of the place matter sooner.

## What the Retailer Is Testing

The convenience chain’s announcement interested me as much as the hardware did. It chose stores where a driver already has something to do, and it plans to watch store visits and how people use the amenities before deciding where to expand.

That’s a retailer treating the stop as part of its business, and it matches what drivers have told us. In [our driver study](/blog/2026/08/24/what-ev-drivers-told-us.html), reliability and charging speed were the two factors respondents chose most often, with clean restrooms close behind. That was a voluntary sample of EV drivers, so I treat it as a signal and no more than that. It still points the same way as these two announcements. Drivers want the charging to be quick and dependable, and then they want the basics around it done well.

## Designing for Both

That’s why we don’t think of the stop as one thing.

Some stops are quick and part of an ordinary day. That’s the idea behind **ChargeVia by Rangeway**, which brings charging to existing businesses where a driver can do something useful in the time they have. What matters there is distance and speed: how far the restroom is from the charger, whether the path is obvious and well lit, and how long it takes to get a coffee.

Other stops come a few hours into a drive, when people want a break whatever the battery says. That was true with a gas tank, too. Our Waystations and Basecamps are being designed for those stops, with a climate-controlled driver’s lounge, premium restrooms, and somewhere comfortable to sit for as long as a guest likes.

With a slow charger, the car decides how long the stop lasts. With a fast one, the guest decides. That’s how it should be.

## What Stays the Same

The engineers are taking time out of the charge. The retailers are putting value into the time that remains. I don’t see those as competing bets, and drivers will benefit from both.

I was too quick last December to say the twenty-minute stop would never change. The rest of that post holds up. However fast the charger gets, someone still has to pull off the road, get out of the car, and decide whether this is a place they’d choose again.

Hotels deal with a version of this every day. Some guests stay a week. Others walk through the lobby once, use the restroom, buy a coffee, and leave. A good hotel operator treats both as guests, because the short visit is often how someone decides whether to come back for the long one.

That’s how we’re approaching faster charging. We’ll keep pushing for quicker sessions, because drivers deserve them. We’ll keep designing for the minutes that remain, because those minutes are where a driver decides whether a stop was worth making. The charge will keep getting shorter. The reason to choose one stop over another won’t.
